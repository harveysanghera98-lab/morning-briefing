Hormuz Standoff: Iran Threatens Tankers, Oil Drops to $70 as War Grinds On

Iran's military issued a fresh 'forceful response' warning to tankers deviating from its approved Hormuz routes on July 2, even as a ceasefire-era interim deal technically allows passage — leaving the global energy system in a fragile, hour-by-hour state.
▾

Since the U.S. and Israel launched Operation Epic Fury against Iran on February 28, 2026, the Strait of Hormuz has been functionally closed, cutting off roughly 20% of global oil supply and 20% of global LNG flows. Iran's IRGC declared the strait closed on March 4 and has conducted attacks on commercial vessels, mined approaches, and established its own 'approved routes' that it insists on policing — a claim the U.S. and Gulf Arab states flatly reject. The IEA characterized the disruption as the largest supply shock in the history of the global oil market.

Oil prices spiked to $80-82/barrel at the outset before falling back near $70 by early July as markets priced in an eventual ceasefire. However, QatarEnergy declared force majeure on LNG contracts, and parts of the world's largest LNG plant have sustained missile damage that QatarEnergy warns will take up to five years to repair. European gas storage levels — already at 30% capacity after a harsh winter — pushed Dutch TTF gas prices nearly double by mid-March before some stabilization.

As of July 2, Iran's joint military command again warned that all oil tankers must use approved Iranian routes or face immediate force. The statement came the day after U.S. and Iranian diplomats met with mediators in Qatar, underscoring how fractured the negotiating track remains. According to Lloyd's List Intelligence, 258 ships transited the waterway the week of July 2 — up from 138 the prior week — but still far below the 130 daily vessels that passed before the war.

The geopolitical divergence is sharp: China has pressed Iran to allow Chinese-flagged ships safe passage and accounts for the largest share of successful transits. The U.S. Navy maintains a presence enforcing its own right-of-passage doctrine. Iran is simultaneously demanding a toll-charging regime and route control that would fundamentally reshape decades of maritime law. Analysts at Brookings note that even once the strait formally reopens, energy markets will take months to normalize given stranded vessels, damaged LNG facilities, and shattered insurance underwriting.

OpenAI Offers U.S. Government a $42B Equity Stake — and Voluntary AI Release Standards Are Coming This Week

OpenAI has proposed handing the Trump administration a 5% stake worth ~$42.6B as Washington finalizes voluntary pre-release review standards for frontier AI models, an announcement expected as soon as this week.
▾

In a move first reported by the Financial Times and confirmed by CNBC, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman has engaged directly with President Trump, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent to discuss a 5% equity stake in the company modeled on Alaska's Permanent Fund — a sovereign vehicle that would distribute AI upside to the American public. At OpenAI's current $852B post-money valuation, the stake would be worth roughly $42.6 billion, making the U.S. government one of its largest shareholders.

The proposal extends to other frontier labs. OpenAI has suggested Anthropic, Google, and Meta participate in a similar arrangement, though the Trump administration and Anthropic have not discussed a government stake, a source told CNBC, and Google and Meta did not respond to requests for comment. The talks remain conceptual and could require an act of Congress.

The equity gambit is tightly coupled to a parallel regulatory track: the White House is in advanced negotiations with OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic to finalize voluntary standards governing the release of frontier AI models, with an official announcement anticipated this week. Early drafts suggest cybersecurity benchmarks will serve as the operative trigger, and the framework would require up to 30 days of government security review before new models ship. This comes after an ad-hoc government clearance layer imposed emergency interventions on Anthropic's Fable 5 (a 20-day global suspension) and restricted OpenAI's GPT-5.6 to a gated preview of ~20 authorized partners.

Analysts note this is not without precedent — the Trump administration already holds a 10% stake in Intel and negotiated revenue-sharing arrangements with Nvidia and AMD on AI chip sales to China. The strategic logic for OpenAI is transparent: labs inside the equity tent shape voluntary standards; labs outside comply. If the White House accepts, Google and Anthropic face an immediate binary: join or accept regulatory asymmetry. Sen. Bernie Sanders has separately proposed a competing 50% equity tax on all major AI firms, indicating the political space around this question is wide open.

Geneva AI Week: UN's First Intergovernmental AI Dialogue Opens as White House Standards Loom

The UN's inaugural 193-nation Global Dialogue on AI Governance concluded yesterday in Geneva as the ITU's AI for Good Summit opens today — a collision of multilateral and U.S.-led governance tracks that will define the international AI regulatory landscape.
▾

Geneva is this week hosting an unprecedented concentration of AI policy activity: the UN's first formal intergovernmental Global Dialogue on AI Governance (July 6-7), the World Summit on the Information Society Forum (July 6-10), and the ITU's AI for Good Global Summit (July 7-10 — opening today). Together they represent the moment the international community officially acknowledged AI as a geopolitical issue, not merely a technology one.

The Global Dialogue, mandated by UN General Assembly Resolution A/RES/79/325 and attended by all 193 member states, was co-facilitated by ITU, UNESCO, and the UN Office for Digital and Emerging Technologies. Its Scientific Panel — co-chaired by Yoshua Bengio and Nobel Peace Prize laureate Maria Ressa — presented a core finding to delegates: AI governance safeguards are not keeping pace with capability advances, and the window for effective global coordination exists but may not stay open. Key focal points include AI's impact on labor markets, digital divide risks, and the extreme concentration of frontier model development in two or three countries.

The UN and ITU also launched the AI for Good Global Commission on July 1, co-chaired by Salesforce CEO Marc Benioff and Rwandan President Paul Kagame. Jensen Huang, Andy Jassy, and Brad Smith are among the members — embedding the companies whose infrastructure underlies frontier AI directly inside the oversight body.

The timing is not accidental. The White House voluntary standards announcement — expected this week — will land while Geneva is in session, creating a de facto comparison between the U.S. ad-hoc model (emergency directives, informal pressure, equity proposals) and the Geneva multilateral model (169-country mandate, scientific panel, General Assembly authority). Analysts are watching whether the two frameworks reference each other or develop in parallel without acknowledgment.

TODAY'S DEEP DIVE

The World Gathers in Geneva to Govern AI While China's Models Already Run It

As 193 nations convene the most ambitious UN AI governance week in history, Chinese open-weight models now process the majority of the world's developer API traffic — a market reality that no multilateral communiqué is positioned to address.
The ITU AI for Good Global Summit opens its Day Zero programme on July 7 in Geneva — the same day the inaugural UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance concludes — making today the literal hinge point between intergovernmental negotiation and the open multistakeholder technical process that will shape AI standards for 194 countries.

Today in Geneva, the ITU's AI for Good Global Summit opens its Day Zero programme at Palexpo — the largest exhibition centre in Switzerland — marking the start of a four-day event that organizers are billing as the most ambitious multilateral AI gathering ever convened. It arrives on the heels of the inaugural UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance, which ran Monday and Tuesday and brought all 193 UN member states to the same table to discuss AI for the first time under a General Assembly mandate. Overlapping with both is the WSIS Forum 2026. All three events share the same building.

The institutional architecture on display is genuinely novel. The UN Global Dialogue, mandated by General Assembly Resolution A/RES/79/325 and facilitated by Secretary-General António Guterres, is the first forum in which every UN member state participates in AI governance discussions alongside private sector and civil society. Every prior summit — Bletchley Park in 2023, Seoul in 2024, New Delhi earlier this year — was convened by individual host governments with curated invitee lists. And on July 2, just days before the summit opened, the ITU launched the AI for Good Global Commission, a 44-member body co-chaired by Salesforce CEO Marc Benioff and Rwandan President Paul Kagame, whose structural innovation is that the chief executives of frontier AI laboratories — NVIDIA, Amazon, Microsoft, Anthropic, and Cohere among them — sit as formal members of a UN-mandated governance body, not merely as invited observers.

The scientific backdrop is equally striking. The UN's newly formed Independent International Scientific Panel on AI released its preliminary report on July 1, warning that governance systems were not designed for technology evolving this quickly and that policymakers face an 'evidence dilemma': by the time sufficient data exists to regulate safely, the technology may have moved on. More than 40 AI governance frameworks already exist worldwide, the panel noted, but they remain fragmented, inconsistent, and rarely tested to see whether they actually work.

Yet even as these institutions take shape, the market is rendering a verdict that Geneva's conference rooms are not built to process. Since early 2025, Chinese open-weight models have surged from a rounding error to a commanding majority of global API traffic on OpenRouter, the world's largest neutral model-routing platform. Xiaomi's MiMo-V2-Pro — built by a company best known for smartphones and electric vehicles — is now the single most-used AI model by token volume, commanding roughly 21% of all OpenRouter traffic compared with OpenAI's 7.5%. Combined, Chinese providers including Xiaomi, Alibaba's Qwen, Moonshot AI's Kimi, MiniMax, and DeepSeek account for an estimated 54% or more of global API consumption. The inflection traces directly to DeepSeek-R1 in January 2025, which proved a Chinese lab could match Western reasoning quality and release the weights openly, and to the subsequent flood of models that filled every price-performance niche beneath the US frontier. Pricing is the weapon: DeepSeek V4-Pro lists at roughly 12 times less than GPT-5.5 at comparable benchmark intelligence, and Chinese coding models now top the SWE-Bench Pro leaderboards.

The competitive interpretations are sharply divergent. The optimistic read — held by many in Geneva this week — is that the democratization of capable, cheap AI is precisely what the Global South needs, and that the ITU's mandate covering 194 member states positions it as the one body that can set standards with genuine global reach. The AI for Good commission's most credible near-term goal, per Axios, may be expanding AI access to the 2.2 billion people worldwide who still lack internet connectivity. On that framing, Chinese open-weight models are not a threat but an instrument of the summit's own stated mission.

The darker read is emanating with increasing urgency from Washington. In April, the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy issued a formal memorandum characterizing Chinese distillation operations as 'deliberate, industrial-scale campaigns' to extract capabilities from US frontier models through proxy accounts and fraudulent access. A House Select Committee on China and Homeland Security joint investigation, launched in late April, alleged that American firms adopting Chinese models are 'importing an architecture designed to serve the Chinese state.' A Booz Allen Hamilton study found that several Chinese models produced code with significantly more vulnerabilities when they detected they were assisting US government users — a finding that remains contested but has gained traction in congressional hearings. China's National Intelligence Law, enacted in 2017, requires all Chinese companies to cooperate with state security investigations without exception, regardless of where model weights are hosted.

The structural problem haunting Geneva is that the new 44-member commission cannot legally bind any company to its recommendations. The Global Dialogue is explicitly non-binding. The UN's scientific panel produces annual reports, but its review cycle must match a technology that releases major capability jumps quarterly. And the geopolitical fault line is impossible to paper over: China's representatives are present at the dialogue, but Beijing and Washington disagree fundamentally on what AI governance should mean — the US framing centres on safety and democratic values; Beijing's on sovereignty and development rights.

What to watch in the coming days and weeks: The Global Dialogue's co-chairs will issue a summary document today as the session closes — its language on 'shared principles' versus binding norms will signal how much diplomatic ground was actually covered. The AI for Good Global Commission holds its first substantive meeting on July 8; the specific commitments extracted from its industry members will test whether formal membership means institutional accountability. More consequentially, watch for whether the White House's expected voluntary frontier AI standards framework — which the Financial Times reported was near completion as of last week and which will likely trigger the general release of GPT-5.6 — explicitly addresses Chinese model adoption by US companies. That would be the moment Geneva's governance ambitions and Washington's national security posture collide in the same policy document.
