Hormuz MOU on the Clock: Doha Talks Close Without Breakthrough as Strait Remains Partially Mined

The US-Iran ceasefire MOU signed June 17 grants Iran only 60 days to guarantee toll-free passage — Doha technical talks just concluded with partial progress, leaving the strait's fate unresolved heading into July 4.
▾

The June 17 MOU signed by Presidents Trump and Pezeshkian commits Iran to making 'best efforts' to ensure toll-free passage through the Strait of Hormuz for 60 days only — the clock is now running. Indirect technical talks in Doha concluded on July 2 without a breakthrough but with incremental implementation steps, including a mechanism to release a tranche of Iran's $6 billion in frozen Qatari-held assets and a communications channel to flag violations. President Trump called them 'very good meetings'; VP Vance said talks are 'going well' while explicitly flagging that the nuclear question is next.

The physical situation at the strait remains precarious. The central traffic separation scheme is still unusable pending clearance of an estimated 80 naval mines, a process the Pentagon assesses could take up to six months. Commercial traffic is routing through two narrow corridors — via Omani territorial waters to the south and an Iranian-controlled northern route — handling far below pre-war capacity. Kpler recorded only 34 vessel crossings on June 30 versus a pre-war norm above 90 daily transits. The International Transport Workers' Federation continues to designate the strait a 'warlike operations area.'

The macroeconomic damage is documented. A Moody's analysis places the war's cost to US families at approximately $100 billion, with average gasoline prices more than $1 per gallon above pre-war levels. The UAE's state oil company estimates full Hormuz flows will not resume until 2027 even with a rapid deal. Brent crude has eased to the low $70s as partial traffic recovery holds, but that remains fragile. WTO data cited by the UK House of Commons Library shows a 95% reduction in crude oil ships and 99% reduction in LNG since the conflict began.

Iranian officials have repeatedly stated they intend to charge 'service fees' on ships transiting, which US Secretary of State has warned 'would make a diplomatic deal unfeasible.' The 60-day clock from the June 17 signing expires in mid-August. Watch for: whether the two sides can agree on nuclear inspections and the fee question before the MOU window closes, and whether Iran re-closes the strait as leverage.

HIGH US Policy & Power

FISA Section 702 Has Lapsed — Surveillance Continues Under Court Certifications Through 2027

Congress failed to reauthorize FISA Section 702 before the June 12 deadline, creating legal uncertainty about the US intelligence community's most heavily used foreign surveillance authority.
▾

Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act lapsed on June 12, 2026, after Congress deadlocked repeatedly. The immediate trigger was Trump's nomination of Bill Pulte — director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency with no national security background — as acting Director of National Intelligence. Democrats, including House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, refused to vote for any reauthorization while Pulte was Trump's pick, citing fears he would 'weaponize FISA information' against political opponents. Even before the Pulte nomination, negotiations had struggled to bridge disagreements over whether backdoor searches of Americans' 702 data should require a warrant.

The practical surveillance impact is more limited than headlines suggest. Under the FISA Amendments Act's transition provision, the FISA Court approved current certifications in March 2026 that run until roughly March 2027, meaning government collection activities legally continue unchanged. Companies are served with directives that remain valid regardless of the statutory sunset. As Cato's analysis notes, 'the program has the FISA court's permission to continue for another year.'

The deeper concern is the oversight architecture, not the collection capability. When Title VII lapsed, the compliance regime — FISC programmatic review, minimization procedures, and congressional reporting — went with the statute. A still-secret March 2026 FISC opinion that Senate Intelligence Committee bipartisan leadership has demanded be declassified reportedly describes serious abuses. Sen. Wyden noted that sensitive warrantless searches of American journalists and political organizations more than tripled in 2025, with the FBI refusing to explain why.

Negotiations for reauthorization continue. Watch for: whether Trump reverses the Pulte appointment or whether Democrats calculate the political cost of blocking a national security authority is too high as the midterms approach.

TODAY'S DEEP DIVE

The World Convenes on AI Governance in Geneva — Without the Only Country That Matters

As 193 nations gather for the UN's first Global Dialogue on AI Governance this weekend, the United States has declared it will not submit to multilateral oversight — leaving China to play the role of responsible global stakeholder.
The UN's first Global Dialogue on AI Governance opens in Geneva on July 6 — in three days — making this the final pre-summit moment to assess what the gathering can actually achieve and what it reveals about the fracturing global order on AI.

On Monday, July 6, diplomats, technologists, civil society groups, and corporate representatives from across the world will gather at Palexpo in Geneva for the first session of the United Nations Global Dialogue on Artificial Intelligence Governance — the most significant multilateral moment on AI since the adoption of the Global Digital Compact in September 2024. The event runs through July 7, overlapping with the ITU's AI for Good Global Summit and the WSIS Forum in what amounts to a concentrated week of AI diplomacy at the heart of international Geneva. The timing gives today's pre-summit period unusual weight: every government is now being forced to declare, or at least signal, where it stands.

The Dialogue was established by UN General Assembly Resolution A/RES/79/325, following the 2024 Summit of the Future. Its mandate is expansive: to bring together all 193 member states alongside the private sector, academia, and civil society to build what organizers describe as a shared foundation for "safe, secure, and trustworthy AI." The agenda clusters around four themes — AI's societal and economic impacts, bridging the AI divide between rich and poor nations, human rights and oversight, and the interoperability of national governance approaches. A preliminary report from the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, co-chaired by Turing Award winner Yoshua Bengio and journalist Maria Ressa, is being formally presented, providing the scientific baseline for deliberations. The UN Secretary-General's message to governments has been unambiguous: "do not wait."

The central drama, however, is not what will happen inside Palexpo — it is what the United States will do. Washington's posture has hardened over the past year into outright rejection of the UN-led process. At a Security Council debate in September 2025, Michael Kratsios, the director of the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, declared that the US "totally rejects all efforts by international bodies to assert centralized control and global governance of AI." The administration's AI Action Plan reinforced the view that multilateral governance efforts tend toward "burdensome regulations" and "cultural agendas that do not align with American values." As recently as February 2026, at a major AI summit in New Delhi, Kratsios repeated that the US "totally rejects global governance of AI." The contrast with prior administrations is stark: the Biden era produced the Bletchley Declaration, the Council of Europe AI treaty, and a US-led UN General Assembly AI resolution — all of which embraced a role for international institutions.

China, by striking contrast, has turned the Dialogue into a diplomatic opportunity. Beijing expressed strong support when the process launched, aligning itself with developing countries and framing global governance as essential to preventing AI from becoming "a game of the club of wealthy nations." China's Global AI Governance Action Plan, published in 2025, casts the United Nations as the primary stage for AI rulemaking, linking governance to bridging the digital divide and building an "inclusive and fair" system. Analysts at CSIS have observed that the US and China have effectively traded traditional roles: Washington now favors de facto dominance through technology diffusion, while Beijing pursues influence through multilateral institution-building.

The non-obvious angle here is the Global South. Developing nations are not passive bystanders in Geneva — they are the prize. China has pursued infrastructure investment and AI capacity-building partnerships across Africa, Latin America, and Southeast Asia, giving it substantial credibility as a champion of poorer countries' interests. The UN itself has had to fundraise separately to help developing country delegates attend the Geneva meeting. Analysts writing ahead of the Dialogue warn against treating the Global South as mere "diplomatic terrain over which influence is exercised"; their priorities — labor displacement, data sovereignty, access to AI tools — may not map neatly onto either Washington's innovation-first posture or Beijing's institution-first framing. The preliminary scientific report is sobering on this point: AI adoption has accelerated broadly but unevenly, with uptake across the Global South lagging far behind the Global North, even as over one billion people now use conversational AI weekly.

Reasonable people disagree sharply about what the Geneva gathering can realistically achieve. Optimists point out that even a non-binding framework — equivalent to what the OECD AI Principles accomplished in 2019 — would establish shared language and create accountability pressure on the largest AI powers. The UN's own special envoy for digital technologies has argued that US skepticism has been "misread" and that there remains room for Washington to exert leadership within an inclusive process. Skeptics counter that without the United States actively constructing the rules, any architecture built in Geneva will be either toothless or captured by norms that suit China's authoritarian model of AI deployment. The Global Digital Compact is non-binding; member states are committed to its principles without any legal obligation to enforce them. There is also the risk that the Dialogue produces what one analyst bluntly called "another UN AI declaration" — process without architecture.

What to watch in the days immediately ahead: whether a formal US delegation appears at all, and in what capacity; whether the scientific panel's report generates consensus language on "catastrophic harm" from advanced AI systems that could constrain future US regulatory autonomy; and whether China uses the platform to push language on open-source AI development that would weaken Western export controls. A second session is scheduled for New York in May 2027, giving governments roughly ten months to decide whether to treat Geneva as a foundation or a footnote. The window for meaningful governance architecture, the panel warns, remains open — but may not stay that way for long.
