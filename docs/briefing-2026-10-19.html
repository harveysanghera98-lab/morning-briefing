URGENT Frontier Tech / AI

Chip stocks suffer worst week since April 2025 on AI-spending doubts

A sharp AI-trade unwind is hitting semiconductors and neoclouds just as the Fed weighs a rate hike.
▾

The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell about 11.4% last week and closed lower for eight straight sessions, per a Portfolio Terminal weekly review. The Nasdaq lost about 4.5% on the week and the S&P 500 fell 1.95% to 7,659.44, its worst week since June. The VIX closed at 19.99, its highest since July 29.

Three catalysts were cited. Broadcom CEO Hock Tan said at an October 12 event in Singapore that AI investors are behaving 'like casino gamblers' and that capital, not chips or memory, may become the next constraint. There was also a report of a more efficient Chinese memory-cell design, and a Gartner forecast that more than $3.5 trillion of AI infrastructure spend could face impairment by 2030. I could only confirm the Tan remarks and the market moves in the sources I retrieved. The Gartner and Chinese-memory items come from a single aggregator and are not independently verified.

The damage was uneven. Eight listed neocloud names fell an average of about 22%, against about 6.6% for Nvidia. Money rotated into defensives, with staples up about 5% and health care up about 3.5%. The Dow rose about 0.7% on the week. Sources diverge on how much of this is a healthy reset versus the start of a larger AI capex reassessment, so watch hyperscaler and chip earnings commentary this week.

What to watch: whether chips stabilize at the open, whether neocloud credit and financing conditions tighten, and how Big Tech capex guidance is received in the remaining earnings season.

URGENT Energy

Oil back above $100 as Iran escalates tanker attacks in Hormuz

Renewed shipping attacks are driving an energy-led inflation shock into a Fed that is now pricing hikes.
▾

November WTI rose 10.4% last week to $101.41, its best week since mid-July, and December Brent gained about 4.9% to $109.86. CNBC, as cited by Portfolio Terminal, tied Friday's jump to Iran stepping up attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz. Oilprice and Kpler data show tanker traffic at multi-month lows.

The backdrop is the US-Israel-Iran war that began in February. Brent peaked near $115 in May, and Iran has been requiring permission and transit fees for passage. A planned G7 release of 100 million barrels has been largely priced in, so it has not capped prices. US crude inventories also fell 8.6 million barrels in the week to October 9.

The inflation transmission is visible. September CPI came in at 3.7% and core at 2.6%, each a tenth above expectations. PPI rose 1.3% on the month. Energy equities (XLE) gained 4.9% on the week.

I could not retrieve this morning's weekend developments, so check for any overnight escalation or diplomatic movement before the open.

HIGH Markets

Fed meets Oct 27-28 with markets pricing a possible hike

Hot inflation has flipped the rate debate from cuts to hikes, with 10-year yields above 5%.
▾

CME FedWatch futures, as reported by Investopedia and cited by Portfolio Terminal, priced about a 17% chance of a hike at the October 27-28 meeting and about 89% by December. The 10-year Treasury yield ended the week near 5.10%, down from 5.24% a week earlier, after touching roughly 5.3% intraday. The decline was attributed to a flight to safety amid the equity selloff.

Stagflation-style pressures are building: headline CPI of 3.7% driven by energy, with producer prices jumping 1.3%. That makes the Fed's choice harder if AI-linked equity weakness deepens, since tighter financial conditions would be arriving from the market side as well.

Watch this week for Fed speakers ahead of the blackout period, Treasury auction demand, and whether the equity selloff changes hike pricing. Exact data-release dates were not confirmed in my sources.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

The Neocloud Is the Fed's Next Problem

Speculative: oil-driven inflation turns the AI credit trade into the first real financial-stability test of this cycle.
EnergyMarketsFrontier Tech / AI
▾

Speculative. The chain runs like this. Hormuz tanker attacks push WTI above $100, September CPI prints at 3.7 percent, and futures now price about 89 percent odds of a Fed hike by December. Higher policy rates land hardest on the businesses that borrowed the most at floating or refinancing-sensitive rates. Neoclouds are the obvious candidates. They buy GPUs on debt, lease them back to hyperscalers and AI labs, and depend on continuous access to private credit. An eight-name neocloud basket already down about 22 percent in a week is not a bad-sentiment signal. It is the market beginning to reprice the cost of their capital.

The bold call: the AI unwind will be decided in credit markets, not in Nvidia's share price. Nvidia fell about 6.6 percent while the neoclouds fell about 22 percent, which tells you equity investors already see the leverage sitting one layer below the chip designers. If a single neocloud has to renegotiate a GPU financing facility or its lenders start demanding higher spreads, that becomes the story. Watch CoreWeave, Nebius and the private-credit desks that fund them, rather than the headline index.

The second-order effect is political. A hike cycle that is triggered by energy and then followed by a credit wobble in AI infrastructure is a combination the Fed has little tolerance for. Expect the Fed to talk about looking through the energy shock while quietly prioritizing financial stability if a major data-center financing deal breaks. That is the trade nobody is pricing: the hike odds may fall not because inflation cools, but because an AI credit event makes a hike unaffordable.

Rare Earths Are the Real AI Chip Lever

Speculative: Beijing lets the November 10 suspension lapse in a way that hits AI, and Washington uses the chip selloff as cover to trade.
ChinaFrontier Tech / AIUS Policy & Power
▾

Speculative. The November 10 expiry of China's suspension of expanded rare-earth export controls is being treated as a routine trade-truce extension question. It should be treated as leverage. A report of a more efficient Chinese memory-cell design added to the chip selloff, which hands Beijing a narrative: its chip stack is improving, and US export controls are less of a moat than Washington assumes. That gives Beijing an incentive to hold the suspension hostage until AI chips and Taiwan are on the table, as the CommBank analysis anticipated.

My bold take: the most likely outcome is a partial extension that is narrow enough to avoid a breakdown but leaves rare-earth licensing for defense-relevant magnets and specialty materials tight. That creates a pressure point for US defense primes and drone makers that are already short on magnets, while the AI trade absorbs the headline risk. Washington's response is the part I would bet on. A falling chip index makes new advanced-chip export restrictions politically cheaper, because the domestic cost looks smaller when Nvidia and the semiconductor index are already down. Expect Commerce to be tempted to tighten controls at the moment Beijing is most sensitive to them, which is exactly when the truce is most fragile.

The unconfirmed part matters here. I could not verify the outcome of the latest Trump-Xi contact or any extension, so this is a read on incentives, not on announced policy. If MOFCOM says nothing before November 10, read silence as a signal that Beijing is keeping the option to reimpose controls on short notice.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

Hormuz Is Quietly Rebuilding the Defense and Power Trade

Speculative: the AI capex money that is leaving chips does not go to cash. It goes to power, maritime defense, and energy infrastructure.
Defense & SecurityEnergyFrontier Tech / AI
▾

Speculative. Tanker traffic through Hormuz is at multi-month lows, a dozen attacks were counted in a short window in late September and early October, and Brent is back near $110. Those are not temporary price spikes. They are the signature of a sustained maritime denial campaign. Two things follow. Navies and their contractors get a durable budget case for escort operations, mine countermeasures and cheap unmanned surface vessels. And the energy shock makes the US and allies less willing to accept a long shipping disruption, which raises the value of anything that reduces dependence on the Gulf.

The bold call: capital rotating out of overextended AI names will not go to cash or staples alone. Some of it goes into the physical bottlenecks AI needs, which are power generation, grid equipment and maritime-security hardware for the energy routes that power the grid. Staples up about 5 percent is the defensive leg. The more interesting leg is energy equities, which rose about 4.9 percent while tech fell. Investors who want AI exposure without GPU-financing risk will buy utilities and gas-fired generation that supply the data centers. That is the trade that survives a neocloud shakeout.

The risk to this read is a diplomatic de-escalation. A Hormuz arrangement that reduces transit fees and attacks would deflate both the energy rally and the defense case in a week. Check the weekend developments before the open, because that single variable flips most of this analysis.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

The Banks Are the AI Bailout Nobody Has Priced

Speculative: capital, not chips, becomes the constraint, and the first lender to mark down AI infrastructure loans becomes the sector's pivot point.
Digital FinanceApplied Tech & IndustryFrontier Tech / AI
▾

Speculative. Broadcom CEO Hock Tan's warning that AI investors are behaving like casino gamblers and that capital may be the next constraint is more useful than the usual chip-stock commentary, because it points at the balance sheet, not the product. If capital is the constraint, the question becomes who is providing it. The answer is a mix of hyperscaler cash flow, private credit funds, bank syndicates, and vendor financing in which chipmakers and cloud providers effectively lend to their own customers. Every layer of that stack looks fine until the cost of money rises, which is the exact scenario the Fed is now pricing.

The bold call: the first major bank to disclose a meaningful markdown on data-center or GPU-backed loans will move the entire AI complex more than any chip earnings report this quarter. Q3 earnings season is already under way with JPMorgan having reported on October 13. Its commentary on loan growth, private-credit exposure and infrastructure lending is worth reading line by line, because banks are where the AI capex story becomes a balance-sheet story. I expect the big banks to say they are comfortable, and I expect the smaller and private lenders to be where the first real stress shows up.

This is the unconfirmed part of the picture. I have not verified any specific bank exposure figures, and the Gartner impairment estimate of more than $3.5 trillion by 2030 comes from a single aggregator. If that number is even directionally right, the market is under-discussing how much of the AI build-out depends on financiers who have never been stress-tested on it. Treat any bank that goes quiet on AI lending during its call as the signal to watch.

TODAY'S DEEP DIVE

Beijing Fences Its Own AI: China Proposes Export Controls on 40 Technologies as the Trade Truce Frays

By putting model weights, agents and computing clusters on a draft export-control list, China is copying Washington's chip-control playbook and putting a month-old truce at risk.
The draft rules were published Friday and their registration requirements take effect immediately, so markets, labs and Washington are only now digesting them as the US-China truce comes under strain.

On Friday, October 16, China's Commerce Ministry published draft export controls covering 40 strategic technologies. Twenty-three of them relate to AI, and the rest span solar and biotechnology. Reporting by Reuters and others says the AI items include models, agents and computing clusters. The reported thresholds reach core technology for models of roughly two trillion parameters or more, along with weights, checkpoints, source code and architecture. The ministry says the aim is the "safety and controllability" of AI. Public comment runs to November 15, after which a final version will follow. Registration duties for certain overseas technology transfers reportedly apply immediately, including to shipments already under way.

The timing matters. Only last month, after Xi Jinping's visit to the White House, Washington and Beijing agreed to cut tariffs on about $30 billion of goods and to open an AI dialogue. Chinese exports rose more than 36 percent year on year in September, partly on AI-related products. Now Beijing is putting its own AI know-how behind a licensing wall. Wendy Cutler of the Asia Society Policy Institute reads it as proof the truce is fragile and predicts a US response with fresh restrictions of its own.

The background is a decade of tit-for-tat. The US built its leverage through controls on advanced chips and chipmaking tools, and China answered with controls on gallium, germanium, graphite and rare earths. The rare-earths truce was extended to January 2027 earlier this month. This draft moves into a new category. It targets the software and know-how layer rather than a physical commodity. It also comes after Chinese open-weight models such as Kimi and DeepSeek spread through Western developers and, most recently, into Apple's China offering through Alibaba's Qwen. Beijing has been the world's main source of frontier-adjacent open weights, and the draft suggests it may no longer treat that as costless.

There are several competing readings. The first is defensive and mirrors the US. Beijing has long objected to Washington's claim that controlling AI capability is a legitimate security tool. Having concluded that frontier models are strategic assets, it is applying the same logic to itself. The ministry's language about misuse and abuse echoes US rationales for chip controls and for earlier, later-reversed US pressure on firms such as Anthropic to bar foreign nationals from some models.

The second reading is that this is leverage ahead of the next round of talks. A broad list with vague thresholds creates discretionary licensing power, which can be tightened or loosened as negotiations require. That is how China used rare earths. Analysts quoted so far stress that control is not the same as a ban. Leonhard Xu of Sinolytics calls the announcement "extremely expansive" but notes that most items can still be exported with simplified licensing. Other accounts say some technologies would be fully prohibited and others would need a permit. That discrepancy is itself a signal that the text is unsettled.

The third reading is about industrial policy. Restricting transfers of technology, IP licensing and changes of ownership could stop Chinese AI, battery and solar firms from being absorbed by foreign buyers or relocating know-how abroad. Lawyers are already advising clients in cross-border M&A and licensing to check whether their deals trigger registration. Southeast Asian and Gulf data-centre buyers of Chinese AI stacks may face new friction.

The open question is open-source. If published weights count as exports, China's strategy of winning global developer share through free models may collide with its own controls. It is more likely that open releases of smaller models stay unaffected while frontier-scale systems are held back. Whether that line holds will show how seriously Beijing means it.

The next milestones are as follows. The consultation closes on November 15, and the final text may land soon after. Watch for US reaction, including any move to restrict Chinese models or cloud access. Watch for the first AI dialogue session under the September deal, and for whether the rare-earths truce survives to January. Watch also for whether Chinese labs delay or change planned open-weight releases. Any of these would show whether this is a bargaining chip or a lasting shift to a fenced-off AI order.
