Global chip selloff deepens as investors retreat from AI

A Bloomberg newsletter dated Oct 15 says the semiconductor selloff widened, which may signal a shift in AI-trade sentiment.
▾

Bloomberg's Oct 15 newsletter says the global chip selloff deepened as investors pulled back from AI-linked stocks. My searches did not retrieve the full article, so I can't give the size of the move, the triggers, or which companies were hit hardest.

The selloff comes with other pressure on the sector. An IDC report seen by Reuters puts Nvidia's share of China's data-center AI chip market at 34% in the first half of 2026, as local rivals filled the gap left by US restrictions. Anthropic and OpenAI are also pushing into custom silicon, which adds to the longer-term question of how much of the AI chip market Nvidia can hold.

Watch for follow-up coverage from the FT, WSJ and Reuters on what drove the move. Also watch whether the weakness spreads to AI infrastructure and power names.

HIGH US Policy & Power

US pushes AI exports at SEMICON West as chip industry flags supplier gaps

Officials are promoting US AI exports and reshoring while executives warn of supplier shortages and higher costs.
▾

Digitimes reported on Oct 16 that officials at SEMICON West promoted AI exports and domestic manufacturing. Industry executives warned about supplier gaps, the cost premium on US-made products, and export controls.

The export-control fight in Washington continues. Three chip-related export control bills are stalled in the Senate. Democratic senators have asked Majority Leader Thune to bring them up and have asked President Trump to stop China buying advanced chips and chipmaking tools. BIS now reviews H200 and MI325X license applications case by case. USTR Greer has said export controls are not part of the US-China AI dialogue.

Reuters reported that the FCC plans an Oct 29 vote on restrictions affecting Chinese labs that test electronics for the US market. That is a proposal and not yet enacted.

Anthropic and OpenAI race to build custom AI chips

Frontier labs are trying to reduce their dependence on Nvidia, and talent is moving between them.
▾

Anthropic confirmed in early August that it is building an in-house team to design custom chips for Claude. It also signed a long-term agreement with Google and Broadcom for TPU capacity, and it has reportedly discussed a custom chip with Samsung. OpenAI is working with Broadcom on a custom inference chip, reportedly called Jalapeño and planned for the second half of 2026. A member of OpenAI's chip team, Clive Chan, has moved to Anthropic.

This is background and not a development from the past 24 hours. It matters because the chip selloff raises the question of how durable the Nvidia-centered AI buildout is.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

Nvidia's China Share Slide Is a Policy Outcome, Not a Market Accident

Speculative: Washington is quietly engineering Nvidia's China exit and letting Chinese rivals take the share
ChinaUS Policy & PowerMarkets
▾

Speculative. The IDC figure that puts Nvidia at 34% of China's data-center AI chip market in H1 2026 is being read as a competitive story. I think it is a policy story. Three chip export bills are stalled in the Senate, and BIS is reviewing H200 and MI325X licenses one case at a time. A stalled bill and a slow license queue do the same thing as a ban: they make Nvidia's China revenue unbankable without anyone having to cast the vote. Nobody in the Senate wants to own the lost China revenue, so the default outcome is that the executive branch gets the blame and the market does the rest.

The selloff is the market pricing that slow-motion ban into the AI trade. Investors are not really reacting to one bad week. They are discounting the possibility that the China leg of Nvidia's growth story is gone for good, and that the local rivals who fill that gap will eventually be cheaper, domestically backed, and hard to dislodge even if controls later loosen. USTR Greer saying export controls are off the table for the US-China AI dialogue reads to me as a signal that the controls are staying, just not on the agenda.

The wrong-footed bet here is that Nvidia recovers its China share once a deal is struck. I expect that share does not come back in this cycle. Watch whether Nvidia's guidance language shifts from China recovery to China as a contained segment. If it does, the selloff was the start of a re-rating, not a dip.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

Broadcom Is the Quiet Winner of the Nvidia Selloff

Speculative: the custom-silicon wave turns Broadcom into the default AI chip hedge and pulls capital out of Nvidia
Frontier Tech / AIMarkets
▾

Speculative. Look at who is actually building the alternatives to Nvidia. Anthropic has a long-term agreement with Google and Broadcom for TPU capacity and is reportedly building an in-house chip team. OpenAI is working with Broadcom on an inference chip reportedly called Jalapeno, slated for the second half of 2026. Samsung has been discussed as a manufacturing partner for Anthropic's chip. Every one of these roads runs through Broadcom's design and integration work, and almost none of them runs through Nvidia's merchant GPU business.

That makes Broadcom the cleanest way to bet against Nvidia's dominance without betting against AI demand. When the selloff is about AI sentiment rather than AI spending, money tends to rotate toward the name that gets paid whether a given lab picks GPUs or custom ASICs. My call is that the next leg of institutional money goes into custom-silicon designers and foundry partners, not into the GPU incumbent that the selloff is punishing. Some of that rotation will look like a bounce in Nvidia, which will confuse people into thinking the thesis failed.

The risk to this view is concentration. Broadcom's customer list is a small number of very large AI buyers, and if any one of them, Anthropic or OpenAI included, decides to pause or delay its chip program, the revenue hole is large. I still think the trade is right. The chip selloff is a sentiment event, and sentiment favors the company that sells the picks and shovels for the people trying to leave the mine.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

The Real Export Control Frontier Is the Test Lab, Not the Chip

Speculative: the FCC's Oct 29 vote on Chinese testing labs is the first move in decoupling electronics certification
US Policy & PowerChinaApplied Tech & Industry
▾

Speculative. Most coverage treats the Oct 29 FCC vote on restrictions affecting Chinese labs that test electronics for the US market as a narrow compliance story. I think it is the opening move in a bigger plan. Chip export controls are hard to enforce because chips are small, fungible, and easy to route through third countries. Testing and certification are the opposite. Every electronic product sold in the US needs a lab report, and whoever controls the labs controls which Chinese-made hardware can clear the US market. A restriction on labs is a restriction on market access that does not require a single new chip rule.

Pair that with what SEMICON West officials were pushing: AI exports, reshoring, and a cost premium on US-made products. Reshoring needs a protected market, and a protected market needs barriers that make foreign-made hardware harder to certify. My read is that the FCC vote, the reshoring push, and the stalled Senate bills are three pieces of one strategy. The chip fight stays stuck in the Senate, so the administration moves the fight to the layer where it can act through a single agency.

The contrarian take is that this will hurt US buyers before it hurts China. Fewer accredited labs means longer certification queues, higher costs for American electronics firms, and a bigger premium on the US-made products that officials are trying to promote. Expect the industry to lobby against the vote on cost grounds, and expect the lobbying to succeed in part. A proposal that makes the policy costlier at home and slower abroad is still policy.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

The Talent War in Custom Silicon Is the Real Reshoring Bottleneck

Speculative: the labs chasing their own chips are poaching the people US fabs and packagers need, and no subsidy fixes that
Frontier Tech / AIApplied Tech & IndustryLabor & Workforce
▾

Speculative. The briefing has two data points that look unrelated: SEMICON West executives warning about supplier gaps and cost premiums on US-made products, and Clive Chan moving from OpenAI's chip team to Anthropic. I think they are the same story. Anthropic is building an in-house chip design team, OpenAI is racing to finish its Broadcom inference chip, and both need a small pool of experienced silicon engineers who have shipped a product before. Those people are scarce, and the labs now have the cash to outbid almost any fab or packaging house for them.

That is the uncomfortable part of the reshoring push. Officials are offering incentives to build capacity in the US, but capacity without skilled people is just empty floor space. The supplier gaps executives described are as much a labor problem as a materials problem, and the frontier labs are pulling from the same talent pool that a US packaging or test buildout would need. I expect the first real casualties of the custom-silicon race will not be Nvidia or Broadcom. They will be the mid-tier US suppliers who cannot match a frontier lab's salary offers and lose their best engineers in a single hiring round.

The bold prediction: within 12 months, at least one major US chip-equipment or packaging supplier will cite engineer poaching by AI labs as a reason for a delayed domestic expansion. If that happens, the reshoring policy will get blamed on trade and tariffs when the binding constraint was who could afford to hire whom.
