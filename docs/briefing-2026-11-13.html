URGENT Markets

Hot inflation pushes 10-year yield to about 5% and revives Fed rate-hike bets

Core inflation rose 0.3% in October, and traders now price a possible December hike, with one Fed governor arguing for a half-point move.
▾

US stocks fell on Tuesday, November 11, after the latest inflation data. The S&P 500 lost 0.4% to 7,520.93, the Dow fell 0.3% to 52,231.89, and the Nasdaq fell 0.7% to 25,065.87. The 10-year Treasury yield jumped to 4.99% from 4.88% the day before. It had fallen since topping 5.30% in early October. Two-year yields had their biggest jump since April 2025, per MarketWatch.

The underlying inflation measure rose 0.3% from September, with groceries and electricity coming in above economists' forecasts. Fed Governor Christopher Waller had said a stronger policy move might be needed if the reading hit 0.3%, and he argued for a half-point hike rather than the usual quarter-point. Traders raised their forecasts for hikes at the December meeting. The reported inflation print and the Fed signalling are the main basis for the hike pricing.

Brent crude fell 1.2% to $91.49 a barrel. It was above $112 at the start of the month because of uncertainty over the war with Iran. Lower oil helps the inflation outlook only if the Strait of Hormuz disruption eases.

To watch: further Fed speakers, Treasury auction demand (TheStreet flagged weak foreign demand at recent auctions) and whether the 10-year holds below 5%. I could not retrieve Reuters, FT or WSJ coverage of November 12 itself, so the figures above run through November 11.

US-Iran war keeps oil volatile and strains Congress

Brent has fallen from above $112 to about $91, but the Hormuz disruption and war-powers fight remain unresolved.
▾

The conflict that began with US-Israeli strikes on Iran earlier this year has repeatedly disrupted Strait of Hormuz shipping. The IEA earlier called it the largest oil-supply disruption in history and approved a 400 million barrel strategic reserve release. Brent was about $91.49 on November 11, down from over $112 at the start of the month.

Search results also indicated the House has for the first time approved a war powers resolution aimed at halting US military action against Iran, and that Trump clashed with Republican senators over it. These items were undated in the results, so verify the timing and vote details before relying on them.

Analysts quoted in earlier coverage said prices would stay elevated even after a deal, because of cargo backlogs, damaged infrastructure and mine clearance. Watch for the Senate's handling of the resolution, any ceasefire diplomacy and tanker traffic data.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

The Coal Orders Are the Real Inflation Story

Speculative: the December hike debate is really a fight over who pays for AI-driven power demand
MarketsEnergyUS Policy & Power
▾

Speculative: the October core print got the headlines, but the administration's reported move to back 13 coal plants under Cold War-era authority is the more telling signal. Washington is treating power prices as a national-security and political problem, and the marginal driver is AI data-center load that utilities are still underpricing. If electricity keeps rising faster than core goods, the Fed will be hiking against a supply shock it cannot fix, which is the worst setup for a central bank's credibility.

My sharp take: Waller's push for a half-point hike is less a forecast than a pressure play. A Fed governor floating a bigger move gives the White House a reason to stop backing fossil plants on political grounds and start talking about grid capacity and efficiency mandates. Watch utilities with heavy exposure to AI-campus contracts, such as Constellation and Vistra, and any Treasury or DOE language tying coal orders to data-center demand. If those utility names reprice before the Fed does, the market has already decided the hike is about electricity.

The 10-year at 4.99% matters because mortgage and corporate-bond pricing follow it, and both are needed to finance the grid build-out the coal plants are supposed to bridge. A December hike would tighten the financing for new transmission and generation just as the administration is trying to make power cheap. That contradiction, not the CPI print itself, is where I would put money on the next policy reversal.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

Hormuz Is a Munitions Problem Wearing an Oil Costume

Speculative: the $91 Brent price is being propped up by a strategic-stock refill cliff and a NATO interceptor shortage
EnergyDefense & SecurityGeopolitics
▾

Speculative: Brent falling from above $112 to about $91 looks like the market pricing a deal, but the IEA's 400 million barrel reserve release is a bridge loan, not a solution. Strategic stocks have to be refilled eventually, and whoever is buying that oil in the first half of 2027 will push prices back up even if Hormuz reopens. Traders who treat this as a clean peace trade are ignoring the refill obligation.

The defense angle is the part almost nobody is pricing. NATO's Rutte says munitions stockpiles are strained while Ukraine aid continues, and an Iran campaign that burns interceptors at this pace leaves Europe with less to give Kyiv. My bold call: the war becomes a contest over interceptor and missile production capacity, and the winners are Lockheed Martin and Rheinmetall, not the oil majors. Any ceasefire that Putin and Erdogan are pushing for would cut the fighting, but it would not restore the stockpiles, so defense orders stay elevated regardless.

The link back to markets is financing. Gulf sovereigns that normally recycle petrodollars into Treasuries may be selling to fund war costs and reserve commitments. That would explain weak foreign demand at recent auctions, which TheStreet flagged, and it would push the 10-year higher on its own. If Treasury auctions keep tailing while Brent stays above $85, the oil story and the bond story are the same story.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

Nvidia Stepped Back, and Now the 5% Yield Is the Real Governor on AI

Speculative: AI lab capex is shifting from equity-backed to debt-backed, and rates decide who survives
Frontier Tech / AIMarketsEnergy
▾

Speculative: Jensen Huang's signal that Nvidia is probably done investing in OpenAI and Anthropic matters more than the Vera CPU benchmark. Vendor-sourced claims of 1.8 times x86 performance are marketing until independent workloads confirm them. The real change is that the labs now have to finance compute with debt and partner capital, and that financing reprices every time the 10-year moves toward 5%.

My sharp take: the 10-year at 4.99% is a bigger constraint on AI capex than any chip roadmap. Circular financing, where a chipmaker funds a customer that buys the chipmaker's hardware, works only while credit is cheap. If the Fed starts pricing hikes, the first casualties will be the debt-funded data-center developers and the neocloud operators, not Nvidia's own balance sheet. Watch high-yield spreads on data-center borrowers and any delays in Anthropic or OpenAI compute contracts as the early warning.

OpenAI's custom Jalapeño chip, slated for deployment by year-end, is its hedge against Nvidia pricing and supply. But custom silicon still needs power and cheap capital to scale. If the grid and the bond market both tighten in the same quarter, the labs with the best long-term contracts will survive and the ones dependent on short-dated financing will be forced to consolidate. Speculative, but I would rather be early on that consolidation than late.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

The Housing Bill Is a Rate Hostage

Speculative: withholding a signature is a bet that the Fed blinks before mortgage rates bite
US Policy & PowerMarketsLabor & Workforce
▾

Speculative: the White House reportedly withholding its signature on a housing bill is being read as a procedural fight, but the timing points to something sharper. A 10-year yield near 5% and a possible December hike make mortgage rates the most politically painful number in Washington. Holding the bill gives the administration leverage over Congress and a story to tell about housing costs while the actual cost driver, long rates, sits outside its control.

My bold read: the hold is a bet that the bill's provisions, probably zoning incentives and financing guarantees, will be rewritten into something that favors the administration's energy and construction priorities. That makes the housing bill a bargaining chip in the same fight as the war powers resolution and the coal orders. Timing on the war powers vote is still unverified, so treat that linkage as a hypothesis, not a finding. Homebuilders such as Lennar and D.R. Horton are the names to watch, because their margins move directly with the 10-year.

For workers, the consequence is labor mobility. Higher long rates freeze the housing market, which keeps people in jobs they would otherwise leave and suppresses construction hiring. If the Fed does hike and the housing bill stalls, the next jobs report will look fine on the headline while the trades quietly contract. That gap between the headline labor data and the housing sector is where the surprise is most likely to come from.
