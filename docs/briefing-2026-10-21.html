OpenAI revenue reset hits chip stocks as it reportedly seeks $30B

A report that OpenAI's annualized revenue is about $50B, not the $68-70B investors assumed, drove an AI selloff, and OpenAI is now reportedly raising at a $1.4T+ valuation.
▾

Chipmakers fell 3.4% on Oct 8 after a report put OpenAI's annualized revenue near $50B, well below the $68-70B figure investors had been using. The gap is attributed mainly to how partner sales are accounted for. The Nasdaq fell 1.25% that day, its worst session since mid-August, and chip stocks are still up more than 80% year to date. These figures come from a CFD broker's blog, so treat them with caution.

OpenAI is now reportedly seeking about $30B at a valuation above $1.4T, per a Motley Fool piece dated Oct 20. The raise and the revenue reset together test whether AI infrastructure spending can keep growing. Nvidia is tied closely to OpenAI. It finalized a $30B equity investment in February and agreed in August to be sole chip supplier at an Ohio data center, with guarantees of up to $105B. Separate reports say OpenAI is designing its own chips with TSMC.

The sources here are secondary and I could not verify them against FT, WSJ or Reuters. Watch for confirmation of the round, any restated revenue figures, and the hyperscaler capex commentary that Microsoft and Alphabet are expected to give on Oct 28.

The $1.4T Round Is a Duration Trade in Disguise

Bond traders at 5.2%, not growth investors, will set what OpenAI is worth
Frontier Tech / AIMarkets
▾

Speculative: the revenue reset matters more than the headline raise. If annualized revenue is near $50B rather than $68-70B, a $1.4T valuation is roughly 28 times revenue instead of about 20 times. That multiple only holds if the discount rate stays low, and it isn't staying low. The 10-year is near 5.2%, the 30-year near 5.6%, and futures were pricing a December hike at about 69% in early October. A company that burns cash for years is a long-duration asset, so every basis point of hawkishness from Warsh comes straight out of its price.

Speculative: the $30B will mostly be strategic money. Nvidia already put in $30B in February, agreed in August to be sole chip supplier for an Ohio data center, and reportedly guaranteed up to $105B. That makes the chip vendor the underwriter of its largest customer's demand. That is circular financing with a nicer name, and when rates bite, circular deals unwind together. The $50B figure comes from a CFD broker's blog, so the whole thesis rests on a number I have not verified.

My call, and I may be wrong: the round closes, but the terms carry structure, such as preferred stock or ratchets, that the headline valuation hides. Secondary-market marks on OpenAI drop before Q1 2027, and the story shifts from model quality to Nvidia's guarantees. The tells to watch are the FOMC on Oct 27-28 and whether Microsoft and Alphabet on Oct 28 talk about capex in dollars or in vague language.

Germany Bought Nvidia's Physical AI Pitch With Energy It Doesn't Have

With Brent above $100, Europe's industrial AI is really a bet on power prices
Applied Tech & IndustryEnergy
▾

Speculative: Merz signing a declaration of intent with Nvidia on physical AI is a photo opportunity with a long tail. The German economy has barely grown since the pandemic, and the AP ties that to high energy prices, Chinese car competition and slow permitting. Brent is around $104 because of the US-Israeli war against Iran. Factories running robots, digital twins and on-premise inference need cheap, steady electricity more than they need a memorandum. Germany's industrial power price is the binding constraint, and no Nvidia partnership changes it.

Speculative: the first real sovereign-compute commitments will not land in Germany's industrial heartland. They will go to the Nordics or France, where hydro and nuclear make power cheap, and the German carmakers and machine builders Huang is courting will run their AI on US hyperscaler capacity hosted abroad. Europe already trails the US badly in data center capacity. Adding a dependency on Nvidia's physical AI stack deepens the reliance that European leaders say they are worried about, while the sovereignty rhetoric stays in the speeches.

My wrong-on-purpose take: by the end of 2027, the most successful European 'physical AI' deployment will be a Chinese or American robotics firm building in a country with cheap power, and Berlin will still be arguing about permitting timelines. Watch for announced data center sites in Germany with actual grid connection dates. If none appear, the declaration was the whole deal.

COBOL Benchmarks Are the Layoff Notice for Government Back Offices

Legacy migration gets cheap just as AI job disruption lands first on women
Labor & WorkforceFrontier Tech / AIAI Regulation & Governance
▾

Speculative: OpenAI published COBOL migration results in which GPT-6.1 Sol scored 86.6% at low reasoning effort, against 5.4% for GPT-5.6 Sol, and 97.9% at higher effort. OpenAI wrote the benchmark itself, so treat the numbers as a marketing ceiling, not a field result. Still, the direction is the signal. COBOL runs tax systems, benefits payments, and core banking ledgers. Migrating and maintaining those systems is the back-office work that has long employed large numbers of clerks, analysts and contractors, and a disproportionate share of those jobs are held by women.

Speculative: a CNBC analysis argues women will be hit first by AI job disruption, citing an ILO finding that nearly one in ten women in high-income countries works in an occupation at the highest AI risk. The mechanism here is not mass layoffs. It is attrition freezes, contractor non-renewals and the quiet end of maintenance budgets in banks and government agencies. Nobody announces the cut, so nobody has to report it. The EU Parliament voted to delay high-risk AI Act obligations to December 2027, and public-sector and credit systems are exactly the category those obligations were written to cover. The migration wave will land before the audit rules do.

My wrong-on-purpose take: the first measurable AI displacement shows up in government and bank payroll data in 2027 as flat headcount in processing centers, and the political fight is about whether the agencies had a choice. Watch for state tax agencies and large banks announcing COBOL retirement programs with no new hiring. Those announcements will tell you more than any lab benchmark.

TODAY'S DEEP DIVE

The Stablecoin Rulebook Is Nearly Written, and the Fight Is Over Who Gets Locked Out

With Treasury's comment window on GENIUS Act implementation closed on October 19, the argument has shifted from whether stablecoins get regulated to whether foreign issuers and yield-bearing products can reach the US market.
Treasury's GENIUS Act comment window closed October 19, and the Blockchain Association's filing was reported October 20, so the rule's contested points are now public.

The news hook is procedural but consequential. On October 19, the comment period closed on Treasury's proposed rule, published August 18, that implements the issuance, offer and sale provisions of the GENIUS Act. Reporting put the count at about 123 submissions. On October 20 The Block reported the Blockchain Association's letter, which broadly backs the proposal but asks for clearer definitions. A separate proposal from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, covering stablecoins issued by OCC-supervised entities, is moving in parallel. The statute's framework is set to take full effect on January 18, 2027, so the window to settle details is short.

The context is that the GENIUS Act was the first major federal crypto statute. It created a licensing regime for payment stablecoins, with reserve requirements and redemption rights. It also left agencies to decide the hard questions: how offshore issuers are treated, what counts as a prohibited yield, and how far liability reaches into the intermediaries that handle these tokens. Those questions decide whether the dollar stablecoin market, which is dominated by a few large offshore-linked issuers, is brought inside the US system or kept at a distance.

The foreign-issuer fight is the sharpest. Treasury's draft uses a reasonable-belief approach to decide when offshore issuance falls outside US rules. Mastercard, per PYMNTS, supports that approach. The Blockchain Association objects to open-ended due diligence on foreign issuers. Its argument is that exchanges, wallets and payment firms often have no direct relationship with the issuer, so it asks regulators to publish prohibition lists they can rely on. Banks want the opposite. They want stronger protection against unlicensed or foreign issuers reaching American customers through intermediaries. One secondary report says no foreign jurisdiction had yet received a comparability determination from Treasury. I could not verify that, but if it is right, the offshore route is effectively closed until Treasury acts.

The second dispute is yield. The Act bars issuers from paying interest solely for holding a stablecoin. Banks fear that rewards routed through exchanges and affiliates would reproduce interest-bearing deposits outside the banking perimeter and drain deposits. The OCC proposal takes a firm line by presuming that yield tied to affiliates or related parties is paid in connection with holding the token. Crypto firms say that presumption sweeps in ordinary loyalty and commercial arrangements. I found no yield-specific comments on Treasury's own proposal, so this fight sits mainly with the OCC.

A third issue is the proposal's knowing participation standard. The Blockchain Association warns it could carry criminal exposure for firms that handle a non-compliant token without knowing it. Defenders say any enforceable regime needs some liability for intermediaries, or the licensing line is easy to evade.

Reasonable people disagree for structural reasons. One camp sees dollar stablecoins as a strategic asset that extends dollar reach and creates demand for Treasury bills, and so wants a permissive door for large global issuers. The other camp sees a threat to bank funding and to financial stability, and wants a strict door. The two camps are using the same statute for opposite aims. The wider macro backdrop sharpens the first view. Long Treasury yields have reportedly hit multi-decade highs, with the 10-year near 5.35%, and stablecoin reserves are a growing buyer of short-dated government debt.

What to watch: Treasury and the OCC must now read and respond to the comments, and final rules would have to land well before January 18, 2027 to give industry time to comply. Watch first for any comparability determinations for other jurisdictions, then for whether the final text softens the knowing participation standard. Also watch whether the OCC keeps its yield presumption, because that choice will signal how far regulators will go to protect bank deposits. Expect final rules in the late-autumn to December window, and expect litigation or lobbying for delay if they are not clear.
