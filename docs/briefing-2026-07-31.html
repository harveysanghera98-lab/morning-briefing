URGENT Geopolitics

US-Iran war widens as drone strike hits Egyptian port

Washington resumed strikes on Iran after Tehran targeted US forces, and the conflict now appears to be spilling into Egypt, whipsawing oil markets.
▾

US forces resumed strikes against Iran on the evening of July 30 after Iran's Revolutionary Guard fired ballistic missiles at American forces in the region; Centcom said the missiles were intercepted. Hours earlier, a drone strike hit Egypt's Mediterranean port of Damietta, setting fire to a US-owned gas storage vessel and a Greek tanker — the first time the fighting has directly touched Egyptian territory. President Trump called the Damietta strike "Iran-related," but the Houthis denied responsibility and several Middle East analysts told reporters they are not convinced Tehran ordered it, leaving the attribution genuinely contested between Washington, Cairo and Sanaa.

The widening conflict has whipsawed crude prices for weeks: Brent jumped above $90 a barrel on Trump's threat to "hit Iran hard," then eased Thursday after Saudi Arabia proposed a naval coalition to protect shipping through the Red Sea and Strait of Hormuz. Egypt has so far avoided direct involvement despite the Suez Canal's exposure, and Cairo is now warning of further "escalation."

Watch whether Egypt is drawn in militarily, whether the Saudi-proposed naval coalition gets traction, and whether Iran retaliates again for the resumed US strikes — each would move oil and risk assets sharply.

HIGH Geopolitics

Suspected Russian missile crashes in Poland, NATO on edge

A Russian Kh-101 cruise missile likely entered Polish airspace during a mass strike on Ukraine, the closest brush with direct NATO territory in months.
▾

Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk said all available evidence points to a Russian Kh-101 cruise missile crashing in eastern Poland after Polish forces detected an unidentified object entering the country's airspace during a large Russian barrage against Ukraine. Tusk, flanked by his interior and defense ministers, visited the impact site and said Poland's air force had been prepared to shoot the object down had it not changed course; it landed in an uninhabited area, causing no casualties.

Tusk said he sees no evidence Poland was deliberately targeted, but the incident revives fears — after previous drone and missile incursions this year — that Russia's war is bleeding into NATO territory. Tusk separately warned Russia could be positioning for a broader "confrontation with Europe" by 2027 and has accused Moscow of plotting sabotage against European airlines.

Watch for NATO's formal response, any Article 4 consultation request from Warsaw, and whether allies use the incident to push through additional air-defense commitments for the eastern flank.

▾

TRACKING AI Regulation & Governance

1,100+ AI lab employees ask Washington to back an international 'pacing' mechanism

Staff at OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta are publicly pushing for coordinated-slowdown infrastructure just as the EU AI Act's core rules are set to bind on August 2.
▾

More than 1,100 employees across frontier AI labs — including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta — signed an open letter circulated July 28 asking the US government to help build the technical and governance infrastructure for a verifiable, internationally coordinated slowdown, to be used if AI capabilities ever outpace the ability to safely oversee them. It is one of the more significant insider-governance interventions of the year, reflecting unease inside labs even as commercial releases accelerate.

The letter lands just before the EU AI Act's core obligations take effect for most in-scope systems on August 2, following the Council's final approval in late June of an "Omnibus" amendment that pushed back some compliance deadlines. In the US, New York state lawmakers separately passed a slate of AI bills this week covering chatbot safety for minors, training-data transparency, and a ban on AI-assisted surveillance pricing — signs that state-level AI regulation is filling the gap left by stalled federal action.

Watch for how labs respond to the pacing-mechanism letter and how smoothly (or not) the EU's August 2 deadline lands for companies operating in Europe.

Europe's AI Gigafactory Blitz Is Bold, Dependent on American Chips, and Years Away

The European Commission's launch of a €30 billion tender for seven AI computing megahubs is the bloc's most muscular assertion of technological sovereignty yet — but the plan's internal contradictions reveal just how far Europe remains from genuine independence.
The European Commission launched the formal tender process for its AI Gigafactory programme on July 30, making this the defining European AI infrastructure story of the week and a direct answer to the US and Chinese compute buildouts that have dominated headlines all year.

On Thursday, July 30, the European Commission formally opened the bidding process for up to seven 'AI Gigafactories' scattered across the bloc, committing €10 billion in public funding from EU and member-state coffers with an explicit target of unlocking at least €20 billion in additional private investment — a total potential mobilisation topping €30 billion. The announcement, framed by Commission President Ursula von der Leyen as Europe's bid to become 'the first AI Continent,' represents the most concrete and financially specific step Brussels has yet taken to compete with the United States and China on foundational computing infrastructure.

The architecture of the programme is layered and deliberately tiered. Four smaller facilities, each requiring at least 75,000 AI chips, can claim up to €500 million in public support; three larger gigafactories, each anchored by at least 100,000 chips, can receive up to €1 billion. The tender window closes November 12, with award decisions expected in early 2027 and construction phased in thereafter. The seven planned sites come on top of the 19 smaller AI factories already operating in various EU member states, suggesting a genuine attempt at cumulative infrastructure build-out rather than a single prestige project. The original plan called for five gigafactories; the expansion to seven came after unexpectedly strong interest from EU governments.

The historical backdrop matters enormously here. The gigafactory concept traces to the Paris AI Action Summit in February 2025, where von der Leyen announced the InvestAI initiative and promised to mobilise €200 billion for AI broadly, with a specific €20 billion envelope for gigafactories. Since then, the programme has been shadowed by a persistent credibility gap: early Commission language promised to 'mobilise' large sums without clearly distinguishing public commitments from aspirational private-sector figures. Thursday's announcement was specifically designed to answer that criticism by specifying the €10 billion public contribution explicitly — though critics note that much of even this public funding remains contingent on approval in the EU's next long-term budget framework, a negotiation that has historically been bruising and protracted.

The plan's central tension is the one Brussels has struggled to resolve for the better part of a decade: technological sovereignty that runs on American hardware. The Commission confirmed it has already signed letters of intent with Nvidia, AMD, and Qualcomm to give gigafactory consortia seamless access to chips. In other words, the physical substrate of European AI independence will, at least initially, be entirely dependent on US semiconductor companies — the very companies whose export decisions, shaped by Washington's chip controls regime, have repeatedly demonstrated how fragile that dependence can be. European chipmakers like ASML supply the machinery that makes these chips possible, but Europe lacks a domestic AI-processor champion at scale.

Reasonable observers disagree sharply about what to make of this. Optimists argue that compute infrastructure is the necessary first step — that without sovereign data centres and training clusters, Europe can never build the model capabilities or attract the AI talent to eventually reduce upstream dependence. They point to the interest from 77 applicants across 16 member states in an earlier informal call as evidence of genuine industrial appetite. Sceptics counter that the timeline is far too slow: with facilities not expected to be operational until at least 18 months after contract signing in early 2027, Europe's gigafactories may not be running until late 2028 or 2029 — by which point the US and China will have lapped the field multiple times. A third camp questions whether public funding at this scale distorts the market rather than correcting it, and whether Europe's regulatory posture under the AI Act — which extended compliance deadlines just weeks ago — will simultaneously deter the very hyperscalers and AI labs whose participation the gigafactories need.

The geopolitical stakes extend beyond the purely economic. The gigafactory programme is implicitly a hedge against a deteriorating transatlantic relationship. If Washington were to tighten chip export controls further, or if a future administration weaponised cloud infrastructure access, Europe's current exposure would be acute. The gigafactory tender is a long-term insurance policy as much as an industrial strategy — though insurance policies purchased on a five-year horizon offer limited protection against near-term shocks.

Watch for three things. First, who actually bids by November 12: whether European-led consortia dominate the field or whether American hyperscalers quietly anchor the proposals will reveal the true balance of power inside the programme. Second, the EU's next multi-year budget negotiations, which will determine whether the public €10 billion commitment is real or aspirational. Third, any US government response — Washington has so far been quiet about a European compute buildout that explicitly aims to reduce dependence on American platforms, but that quiet will not last forever.
