URGENT Markets

Fed holds at 3.75-4.00% in a 10-2 split, with two dissents for a hike

Warsh's Fed paused after September's first hike since 2023, but two officials wanted to tighten again and December is still open.
▾

The Federal Reserve held its target range at 3.75% to 4.00% on October 28. Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack dissented in favor of a quarter-point hike. The September 16 hike was the first increase since 2023.

Chair Kevin Warsh said that earlier move is starting to work and that inflation expectations are moving the right way. He rejected suggestions that the November midterms influenced the decision, saying politics played no role. He also said he does not favor forward guidance outside a crisis, so December was left open without a commitment.

Warsh also announced that the Fed will hold fewer rate-setting meetings starting in 2027. Investors reacted to the change. One analyst quoted by CNBC expects a hike in December and another in early 2027. Prediction markets earlier priced a December hike at roughly 72%, though that figure predates the decision.

Watch the next inflation and jobs data, the December 8-9 meeting, and how the fewer-meetings change is structured. I could not retrieve the market reaction in detail.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

The Fed Just Priced Its Own Capex Problem

Speculative: a December hike turns the $800B AI buildout from a growth story into a refinancing story.
MarketsApplied Tech & Industry
▾

Speculative read: the market is treating this week's Fed hold as a pause, but the 10-2 vote says the committee is already fighting over the next move. Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack wanted a hike now, and Kevin Warsh refused to promise anything for December. That is a chair who would rather keep the option open than give hyperscalers a clear rate path. Meanwhile Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon are being asked to justify more than $800 billion in capex, much of it financed with debt and long-dated leases. Every quarter of higher-for-longer rates raises the hurdle those projects have to clear.

My bold call: if Microsoft or Amazon raise capex guidance this week and December comes in hot, the first crack will show up in data center financing, not in chip orders. Watch for hyperscalers shifting toward off-balance-sheet structures, private credit deals and lease commitments, because those are the instruments that get expensive first when the discount rate moves. Nvidia's order book would look fine right up until a financing partner gets repriced. The Azure growth figure of about 45% that analysts are focused on matters less than whether anyone is willing to fund the next 45%.

The wrong-footed part of this take is the politics. Warsh said the midterms played no role. Given that Warsh's Fed is shortening its meeting calendar starting in 2027, it is fair to ask whether fewer, more telegraphed meetings reduce the central bank's flexibility to respond to a capex-driven credit event. This is speculation, and the Q3 results were not retrieved in this briefing, so treat every number here as a preview to be checked.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

Chips Were Left Off the Table, So Samsung Becomes the Hedge

Speculative: the export-control gap in the US-China truce is pushing frontier labs toward non-US fabs and Beijing toward its own silicon.
Frontier Tech / AIChinaUS Policy & Power
▾

Speculative read: Jamieson Greer confirmed that export controls on advanced AI chips and chipmaking tools were excluded from the US-China talks, and the truce now runs to January 10, 2027. The tariff track is being handled by a Board of Trade covering about $30 billion of goods in each direction. The semiconductor track has been carved out entirely. That leaves the most important variable in AI, which is who can get leading-edge compute and from where, unresolved while both governments celebrate the trade deal.

My bold call: Anthropic's reported talks with Samsung about a custom chip are not a hedge against Nvidia alone. They are a bet that the next export-control shock will hit Taiwan-made capacity, and that a Korean foundry relationship gives a US lab a second supply line that Washington is unlikely to block. Expect Congressional China hawks to pressure Samsung's US fab footprint and any licensing arrangement before they touch the broader truce. If that happens, the January 10 expiry becomes a chip deadline in disguise, and the Board of Trade becomes the place where tariff relief is traded against chip concessions.

The wrong take I would stake a reputation on: Beijing's response will not be to buy more American coal or soybeans to keep the truce alive. It will be to accelerate domestic accelerator programs and treat any new US chip restriction as confirmation that the truce was never meant to last. The Trump-Xi summit produced spectacle, and the chips were the part nobody wanted to discuss. That silence is the signal.

TODAY'S DEEP DIVE

Same Night, Opposite Verdicts: Wall Street Starts Pricing the Funding Cost of the AI Build-Out

Microsoft was rewarded and Alphabet punished for the same AI spending boom, which suggests investors now care less about cloud growth and more about who can pay for it.
The split reaction to Microsoft and Alphabet earnings came hours after a hawkish Fed hold, and Amazon reports today.

On Wednesday evening, Microsoft and Alphabet reported quarterly results within minutes of each other. Their shares then moved in opposite directions. Microsoft rose roughly 4 to 5 percent after hours. Alphabet fell about 10 percent.

The headline numbers do not explain the gap. Google Cloud revenue roughly doubled, up 103 percent to about $30.7 billion, helped by sales of TPU systems. Azure grew 48 percent, beating Microsoft's own 45 percent forecast, and Microsoft guided to 49 to 50 percent next quarter. Demand is not the worry. Both companies report huge contracted backlogs: $677 billion at Microsoft and $561 billion in Google Cloud commitments.

The difference is in the cash. Alphabet spent $51.4 billion on capex in the quarter against operating cash flow of $42.3 billion. Free cash flow was negative $9.1 billion, the second negative quarter in a row. It filled the gap with $28.7 billion of bonds and $12.4 billion of new shares, and it bought back no stock. It kept its 2026 capex range at $195 to $205 billion and said 2027 spending will increase significantly, with no figure given. Microsoft produced $18.6 billion of free cash flow, issued no bonds, returned $10.2 billion to shareholders and trimmed its 2026 capex plan slightly because some GPU deliveries slipped into 2027. Alphabet's core business also disappointed. Search grew 12 percent and YouTube ads about 9 percent, both below estimates, and about $8.7 billion of equity-investment losses cut earnings per share by roughly $0.55.

This matters because of how the trade has been framed. Through the summer, the debate was whether hyperscalers would show revenue to justify the spending. That question is partly answered: cloud revenue is accelerating sharply. A second question is taking its place. If the biggest platforms must borrow and issue equity to keep building, AI infrastructure starts to look less like a self-funding monopoly rent and more like a capital-intensive utility business. In that business, returns depend on the cost of capital.

That cost is rising. On Wednesday the Federal Reserve held its target range at 3.75 to 4.00 percent in a 10-2 vote. Governor Cook and Cleveland Fed President Harmer dissented in favor of a quarter-point hike. The statement upgraded the economy from a solid to a strong expansion, and Waller did not rule out further tightening. After a September hike, markets now treat December as live. Higher rates raise the price of the bond financing Alphabet is relying on, and they raise the discount rate applied to cash flows that are mostly expected years from now.

There are three reasonable readings. The bullish one says the market is overreacting. Alphabet is supply constrained, is renting third-party capacity and has more orders than it can serve. Negative free cash flow in a land grab is a sign of strength, and the backlog will turn into revenue. The cautious reading says backlog is not cash. Only about 30 percent of Microsoft's commitment falls due within twelve months, and customers who are themselves unprofitable AI labs may not honor long-dated contracts. This echoes earlier worries about circular financing and OpenAI's restated revenue. The third reading is about competition. Microsoft's discipline, in part forced by delivery delays, may be an accident, and its larger spending is still to come, with capex above $50 billion expected next quarter. Alphabet may simply be first to hit the funding wall that others reach later.

There are also caveats. After-hours moves often shrink or reverse by the open, reported moves vary by outlet, and Alphabet's earnings figures differ depending on whether adjusted or GAAP numbers are used.

To watch: Amazon reports today, and its free cash flow and financing mix will show whether the market is sorting hyperscalers by balance sheet. Alphabet's 2027 capex figure is the key missing number, likely at the next quarterly report or a mid-quarter update. Corporate bond issuance volumes in November will show how much debt-funded AI the credit market will absorb. The Fed's December meeting is the macro hinge: a hike would tighten the financing conditions that Wednesday's results just made central.
