Gaza strikes intensify as Trump's peace plan wobbles, Iran talks swing between 'imminent' and 'duplicitous'

Two of Trump's signature Middle East peace efforts are fraying simultaneously, with real casualties and real deal risk this week.

HIGH US Policy & Power

New Section 301 tariffs on 60 countries take hold as markets await Friday's jobs report under Fed Chair Warsh

Washington replaced the tariffs the Supreme Court struck down with a new legal theory, and Friday's payrolls data will shape the Fed's September call.
▾

After the Supreme Court's February ruling that IEEPA does not authorize the president's global tariffs, the administration has pivoted to Section 301 of the Trade Act, imposing new 10-12.5% tariffs on 60 trading partners — covering 99.4% of US imports — justified by findings that those countries failed to enforce forced-labor import bans. The new duties took effect just after midnight on July 24 and represent an attempt to make the tariff regime more legally durable than its predecessor, though the average per-household cost is already running near $900 a year, according to Tax Foundation estimates.

All of this lands the same week the Bureau of Labor Statistics releases the July jobs report Friday morning, the first major labor read for new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, who was confirmed by the Senate in a historically close 54-45 vote in May and took office May 22. Markets are watching for whether cooling employment data — echoing weak prior prints tied to tariff-driven manufacturing and logistics softness — pushes the Fed toward a September rate cut, with some analysts now floating a 50-basis-point 'catch-up' cut. The 10-year Treasury yield sitting near 4.73% suggests the bond market isn't yet pricing in aggressive easing.

Where coverage splits: legal and trade outlets (Sidley, WilmerHale, Skadden) are focused on whether the Section 301 approach survives fresh court challenges the way IEEPA didn't, while financial press is more focused on the labor-market and rate-cut angle. Watch Friday's payrolls print and any early legal challenges to the Section 301 tariffs this week.

HIGH AI Regulation & Governance

EU begins enforcing AI Act transparency rules, but heavier high-risk regime slides to 2027

Europe's AI law just went from paper to enforcement for the first time, though a late 'Digital Omnibus' softened what's actually due this year.
▾

As of August 2, the European Commission's AI Office and national authorities began enforcing Article 50 transparency obligations under the AI Act — requiring providers and deployers of certain AI systems to disclose when users are interacting with AI or viewing AI-generated content. Noncompliance can trigger fines up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover. The Commission adopted implementing guidelines on July 20 to clarify scope ahead of the enforcement date.

But a late amendment package, the 'Digital Omnibus on AI,' has effectively split the AI Act's rollout into two speeds: transparency rules proceed on the original schedule, while the far more consequential high-risk-system regime — covering things like AI in hiring, credit, and law enforcement — has been pushed more than a year out, with AI regulatory sandboxes now not required until August 2027. The grace period for labeling AI-generated content was also shortened from six months to three, with a new deadline of December 2, 2026.

This is the most concrete AI governance action globally this week, and it matters as a signal for how the EU balances its 'Brussels effect' ambitions against industry-competitiveness pressure — the Omnibus was widely read as a concession to lobbying that the original timeline was unworkable. Watch for how US AI labs providing services in the EU adjust disclosure practices in the coming weeks, and whether other jurisdictions cite the EU's phased retreat as precedent for their own delays.

TRACKING China

China's export-led growth model is drawing louder overcapacity warnings from trade partners

Beijing is leaning harder on exports to mask weak domestic demand, and analysts say that's setting up broader tariff retaliation beyond the US.
▾

China's Q2 GDP grew 4.3% year-on-year, per National Bureau of Statistics data released July 15, but fixed asset investment fell 5.7% in the first half of 2026 — a sign domestic demand remains weak even as headline growth holds up. Analysts cited by The Epoch Times argue Chinese manufacturers are increasingly dumping excess output abroad to compensate, a dynamic most visible in solar: Tongwei, LONGi and TCL Zhonghuan are projected to report combined first-half losses exceeding 10 billion yuan ($1.48 billion) due to oversupply.

The risk flagged across trade-focused coverage is that this pattern — expanding capacity faster than demand, then exporting the surplus — is what's driving both the US's new Section 301 tariffs and similar defensive measures from other economies, since China's rising manufacturing trade surplus increasingly squeezes developing-world competitors as well as the US and EU.

This is a slower-moving structural story rather than a single news event, but it's the throughline connecting this week's US tariff action to broader China-linked trade tension. Watch for any retaliatory Chinese measures in response to the new US tariffs, and further earnings from Chinese solar and EV manufacturers this month as a read on how deep the overcapacity problem runs.

TODAY'S DEEP DIVE

Palantir's 93% Revenue Explosion Is the Most Important Data Point in Enterprise AI Right Now

Palantir's blowout Q2 results — $1.94 billion in revenue, US commercial growth of 149% — confirm that the long-awaited enterprise AI adoption wave has arrived, and that 'AI sovereignty' is the product the market actually wants to buy.
Palantir reported its Q2 2026 earnings after the bell on Monday August 3rd, sending shares surging in after-hours trading and making it the defining enterprise AI story of the week.

For years, skeptics questioned whether the torrent of AI capital investment would ever translate into genuine enterprise revenue. On Monday evening, Palantir provided the most emphatic rebuttal yet. The company reported second-quarter 2026 revenue of $1.94 billion — a 93% year-over-year surge — while US commercial revenue rocketed 149% to $764 million. Net income hit $1.07 billion, and the company raised its full-year 2026 revenue guidance to $8.15 billion, a near-$500 million upward revision. Shares jumped more than 12% in after-hours trading. The results land at a pivotal moment: just as investors have begun asking hard questions about whether AI capex cycles produce real returns, Palantir is delivering numbers that demand serious attention.

What makes the Palantir story structurally distinct from the broader AI hype cycle is its product concept. The company does not build large language models. Instead, its platforms — Gotham for defense and intelligence, Foundry for enterprise data orchestration, and AIP for deploying AI inside secure, sovereign environments — serve as the connective tissue between frontier models and institutional reality. As one analyst framing put it, if a model is a powerful engine, Palantir is the steering wheel, dashboard, brakes, and road network that let organizations use it safely. The bet is not on any single model winning; it is on enterprises needing a durable, auditable layer to operationalize whichever model they choose.

The concept CEO Alex Karp is aggressively marketing is 'AI sovereignty' — the idea that governments and large enterprises will not entrust their most sensitive data and workflows to frontier labs whose alignment, reliability, and long-term commercial terms remain uncertain. Karp has been unusually candid about his skepticism of those same labs. On CNBC Monday, he reiterated that frontier labs are too untrustworthy for enterprise deployment, and publicly called for robust competition in open-weight models, joining a letter urging the government not to restrict them. His logic is strategic: more models mean more demand for a neutral orchestration layer, and Palantir wants to own that layer.

The geopolitical dimension sharpens this further. US government revenue hit $809 million in Q2, up 90% year-over-year, driven by defense, intelligence, and public safety agencies expanding AI investments — including the Maven Smart System, now a formal Pentagon program of record. The combination of soaring commercial and government demand tells a coherent story: American institutions across sectors are concluding that AI deployment at scale requires a trusted intermediary with security clearances, data governance controls, and proven battlefield credibility. Palantir is, for now, the only company with all three.

But the bull case is not unchallenged. Palantir's valuation remains extraordinary by any conventional measure — forward price-to-earnings above 100x and price-to-sales north of 60x, among the highest in enterprise software. Shares are actually down roughly 30% year-to-date, meaning Monday's after-hours surge represented the market catching up to the operating reality, not initiating a fresh stampede. Hyperscalers including Microsoft and Amazon are pushing aggressively into enterprise AI orchestration, and OpenAI and Anthropic are extending beyond pure model provision into deployment tooling — all territory Palantir considers its own. International government revenue has also lagged, with some European allies exploring domestic alternatives, a meaningful vulnerability for a company that frames itself as the answer to AI sovereignty concerns.

The deeper question Palantir's results force is whether 'AI sovereignty' is a durable category or a transitional wedge. The optimistic reading is that data sensitivity, regulatory complexity, and national security imperatives will permanently require a specialized intermediary — that frontier labs will never be allowed inside the most valuable institutional workflows. The pessimistic reading is that as models become commoditized and hyperscalers extend their compliance certifications, the value of the orchestration layer compresses, and Palantir's current growth rate reflects a one-time catch-up rather than a sustainable moat.

What to watch: Palantir's Q3 guidance of $2.16 billion implies growth acceleration continuing through autumn. The critical variable is whether US commercial deal velocity — 220 deals above $1 million closed in Q2, including 70 above $10 million — is sustainable or front-loaded. Watch also for how Microsoft's and Amazon's enterprise AI tooling announcements in Q3 position against AIP, and whether any European sovereign AI initiative begins to credibly challenge Palantir's government footprint outside North America. If growth holds above 80% through year-end, the debate about AI's commercial reality will be effectively closed. If deal sizes start to shrink, the sovereignty narrative will face its first real stress test.
