URGENT Geopolitics

Houthi strikes on Riyadh airport push Trump toward joining Saudi fight

The deadliest attack on Saudi Arabia in a decade has Washington weighing direct military involvement, with Brent above $106.
▾

Houthi forces struck King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh at the weekend. Reports put the death toll at 12 to 13 with hundreds wounded, and the airport has now been hit several times in recent days. Trump called the attack unacceptable after a call with Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman. He said he is weighing whether to join Saudi strikes on the Houthis, and he blamed Iran for trying to drive up oil prices. He has not committed the US to the campaign.

The Houthis have threatened to hit Saudi Aramco facilities and the Riyadh airport again if Saudi Arabia strikes Sanaa. They also warned of a swift and painful response if the US joins. The US Embassy in Riyadh now requires special authorization to use airports in the kingdom. Saudi forces have launched new strikes and a coalition is intercepting missiles.

Brent closed Monday at $106.65 and WTI at $93.74, and prices eased slightly on Tuesday. This comes on top of an ongoing US-Iran conflict, which makes the energy shock a driver of inflation and Fed policy. Trump has predicted oil will drop sharply once the Iran war ends. The sources I retrieved were CNBC, NBC, Fox and CBS, and I did not get FT, WSJ or Reuters coverage, so I can't compare how those outlets frame it.

Watch for a US decision on joining strikes, any hit on Aramco infrastructure, and Gulf airspace restrictions.

A hot September print could revive the bond selloff after the Fed's surprise hike.
▾

Bloomberg reports the 10-year Treasury yield has hovered around a 24-year high. Another source I retrieved put the latest close at 5.209%, which I could not confirm independently.

Fed Chair Warsh's comments are also being watched. The energy shock from the Iran conflict and Saudi attacks is the main pass-through risk to inflation. Positioning raises the risk that a hot print triggers a sharp move.

I did not find the actual CPI release in my searches, so treat these figures as pre-release expectations.

Memory chip stocks slide for a second day on China and AI-financing worries

CXMT's progress and warnings on AI capital access hit SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron.
▾

Memory stocks fell for a second straight session on Tuesday. Micron dropped roughly 4-5% and SK Hynix fell more than 10%. Samsung lost 7.4% amid a rout in South Korea's KOSPI. Reports cite China's CXMT announcing progress on a denser memory-cell design, which could intensify competition.

Commentary also tied the move to warnings from Intel and Broadcom CEOs that access to capital could become a headwind for the AI buildout. Broadcom CEO Hock Tan's 'casino' remark on AI investing added to the nervousness. A possible labor strike in Taiwan was also mentioned.

The broader market held up. The S&P 500 was flat at 7,805.45 and the Nasdaq fell 0.15% to 27,204.38. The Dow lost 0.35% to 51,670.10. Chip stocks recovered most of their intraday losses by the close.

Watch whether the South Korean selloff continues and how upcoming chip earnings treat AI capex financing.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

The Riyadh airport strike is the Fed's real rate decision

Speculative: a Houthi-driven oil shock makes a December hike the base case, and the late-October hold is the thing to fade
EnergyMarketsUS Policy & Power
▾

SPECULATIVE. Brent above $106 is not a side story for Fed Chair Warsh. It is the inflation print that gets to decide policy. The market is already pricing a hold in late October and a hike in December. My read is that the hold is the soft part of that path. If the Houthi campaign keeps hitting Saudi infrastructure and Washington actually joins the strikes, headline inflation keeps rising into the fourth quarter and the Fed has little room to wait. The 10-year sitting near a 24-year high, reportedly around 5.2% in one source I could not confirm, makes the trade crowded on the other side.

JPMorgan is the early warning, and most people are reading it backwards. Its $658 million in securities losses from the bond rout is what a bank looks like when it holds long duration and the yield curve keeps repricing. Dimon's line that economic forces 'shift under the surface' reads to me as a man who sees the mark-to-market hit coming through the next quarters. Goldman's 72% jump in stock-trading revenue is the hedge that volatility pays for. The split is not a story about two banks. It is a story about who is long rates and who is paid to trade them. If the Riyadh strikes escalate, expect more securities marks at the big banks and more trading income at the desks that run the hedges.

The bet: today's CPI matters less than the next two weeks of Gulf headlines. A hot print plus an Aramco hit would push the December hike from a possibility to the consensus. The soft jobs report is the only thing holding the selloff back, and a bad Houthi week is enough to erase that cushion.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

CXMT is the memory-chip threat, and AI financing is the second shoe

Speculative: the memory selloff is about who can afford the next HBM cycle, not just China's cell design
Frontier Tech / AIChinaMarkets
▾

SPECULATIVE. The market is treating CXMT's denser memory-cell design as a competitive headline. I think it is a pricing headline. Chinese memory makers do not need to beat SK Hynix on leading-edge HBM to hurt it. They only need to flood the commodity tiers and make Samsung and Micron defend capacity they already committed to. SK Hynix falling more than 10% and Samsung down 7.4% suggests Korean investors are pricing in margin compression, not a lost technology race. Micron's 4-5% drop fits the same read.

The second dot is the financing warning. Intel and Broadcom CEOs both flagged access to capital as a possible headwind for the AI buildout, and Hock Tan's 'casino' remark landed on the same day. Put that next to a 10-year yield near 24-year highs and you have a cost-of-capital problem for anyone who has promised to buy memory on a multi-year schedule. Memory makers are the most exposed link, because they sign capacity deals against demand that depends on hyperscalers borrowing cheaply to build data centers. If financing gets tighter, the first orders to slip are the ones with the longest lead times, and those are memory wafer starts.

The odd piece is OpenAI's chip lead Clive Chan reportedly leaving for Anthropic, alongside Anthropic's in-house chip team. My speculative take: the labs want their own silicon roadmaps so they stop being price-takers for merchant HBM and accelerators. If that works, the winners are whoever sells the design-to-tape-out services and the losers are the merchant memory vendors whose pricing power rested on everyone needing the same parts. Watch whether the South Korean selloff continues into the next chip earnings, and whether anyone names a financing cost as a reason to cut capex guidance.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

Gulf petrodollars may be the AI buildout's marginal lender, and the Houthis just raised their price

Speculative: airport closures and Aramco threats make Gulf capital for AI projects riskier at exactly the moment it is needed
GeopoliticsEnergyFrontier Tech / AI
▾

SPECULATIVE. Oil at $106 Brent is a windfall for Saudi Arabia, and the Saudi sovereign fund has spent the last few years positioning itself as a financier of the AI and data-center build. Most people treat those two facts as separate. I think they are linked. When the US Embassy in Riyadh requires special authorization to use the kingdom's airports, that is a signal that foreign executives, engineers and lenders now carry a war-risk premium in Riyadh. That premium lands on any project that needs people on the ground to close deals and run hardware.

The rest of the chain is familiar. Broadcom's and Intel's warnings about access to capital are about who will lend against AI capex. If Western banks are already absorbing securities losses from the bond rout, as JPMorgan's numbers suggest, they have less appetite for long-dated project loans. A Gulf fund that is flush with oil cash but nervous about missiles over Riyadh becomes the swing lender. My bet is that it slows down or re-prices its AI commitments until the airspace question is settled, and that the first sign will be quiet delays rather than announced cancellations.

A bolder version: the Houthi campaign may do more damage to the AI buildout than any chip design, because it raises the cost of the marginal dollar. Watch for any Gulf-linked data center or AI infrastructure announcement that gets a new timeline or moves to a different jurisdiction, and for Aramco-related threats becoming real strikes. If either happens, the CXMT story and the financing story stop being separate headlines.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

Trump needs a Gulf ceasefire before November more than he needs a war

Speculative: 'oil will drop once the Iran war ends' is a midterm promise, and the deal, when it comes, will be sold on gasoline prices
US Policy & PowerEnergyLabor & Workforce
▾

SPECULATIVE. Trump is weighing whether to join Saudi strikes on the Houthis, and in the same breath he blamed Iran for trying to push oil higher and said prices will fall sharply once the Iran war ends. Read those together and the calculation looks domestic. The midterms are in early November. Gasoline prices at this level are a campaign problem, and a weak recent jobs report makes the affordability story worse. My bet is that the administration's real preference is a fast de-escalation that it can announce as a win, and that direct US entry into the Houthi campaign is the threat it uses to bring Riyadh and Tehran to the table, not the outcome it wants.

The labor data supports the squeeze. A soft jobs report reduces the Fed's appetite to hike, but an energy shock is a tax on the same households whose paychecks are slowing. If the White House can cut oil by $20 a barrel with a deal, it gets a cleaner inflation print, a Fed that holds rather than hikes, and a yield curve that stops hurting bank securities books. That is a bullish chain for Treasuries, and a bearish one for the energy names that have been pricing permanent disruption.

The tell to watch is not the strikes. It is the rhetoric. If Trump starts talking about oil and the Houthis in the same breath as a deal timeline, or if the US Embassy in Riyadh loosens its airport authorization, the ceasefire trade is live. If he commits US aircraft and the Houthis keep hitting Riyadh, then this stops being a midterm story and becomes a sustained stagflation story, and the bond market will not be patient about it.
