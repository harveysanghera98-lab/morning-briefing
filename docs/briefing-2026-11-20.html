Trump, Xi and Putin meet in Shenzhen, with little to show for it

The first three-way meeting of the US, Chinese and Russian leaders took place on the sidelines of APEC, and reports say it produced few concrete results.
▾

Trump, Xi and Putin met in Shenzhen on the sidelines of the APEC summit. Trump met Putin for 42 minutes and Xi for about an hour before the three-way session. Chinese officials called it a 'trilateral meeting' rather than a summit.

Ukraine, Iran and nuclear risk were reportedly on the agenda. The Kremlin's readout said the leaders agreed to boost oil and gas production and 'reaffirmed that there can be no winners in a nuclear war'. It also said they would keep talking about reducing nuclear risks. China has never agreed to limits on its arsenal.

CBS News said the meeting appeared to achieve little. The Washington Post framed it as Xi and Putin sharing the stage with a weakened Trump, and as a sign of a shifting global order. The Globe and Mail called it historic, which shows the framing split between 'historic' and 'hollow'.

Trump leaves China and is due to brief the press Friday. Watch for any follow-up on Ukraine talks, chip export controls and tariffs. Also watch how Congress, where Democrats now hold at least one chamber, reacts.

Coverage this morning was thin and came mostly from US broadcasters and newspapers. I could not retrieve the FT, WSJ or Reuters versions.

Shenzhen Was a Chip Deal Dressed as a Summit

The only concrete output was the oil pledge, and the real currency is export controls.
GeopoliticsChinaEnergy
▾

Speculative: the coverage calls Shenzhen a flop, and that's the wrong read. The Kremlin's readout had one hard item, a pledge to raise oil and gas production, and a leader who needs to show he can move oil prices before a Congress that now holds at least one chamber is not making a soft gesture. My bet is the quiet trade involved the remote-access chip rules. Beijing gets a slower enforcement timeline on those rules, and Washington gets Russian barrels and Chinese pressure on Tehran over Hormuz transit fees that have throttled supply for months. The Iran item is the tell: Iran was reportedly on the agenda, and a Hormuz outcome would show up in Brent before any official says so.

The framing split the briefing flagged, historic versus hollow, is a distraction. Nuclear 'no winners' language costs nothing and binds nobody, and China has never accepted arsenal limits, so the nuclear section was theater for the Western press. Trump gets a photo with Xi and Putin, which is what he came for, while the substance moves through agencies that do not brief the press Friday.

Watch the Friday briefing for any mention of chip licensing or tariff pauses, not Ukraine. If Commerce quietly loosens or delays the remote-access rule within 60 days, this thesis is right. If it tightens, I am wrong and Shenzhen was hollow in the literal sense.

The Memo Is the Opening Move in a Losing Subpoena Fight

Scharf's sign-off rule makes the must-pass defense bill the only leverage a new Democratic chamber will have.
US Policy & PowerDefense & Security
▾

Speculative: the White House counsel memo requiring counsel and Office of Legislative Affairs approval before agencies testify is a pre-emptive wall, and Democrats who go to subpoenas first will lose. Subpoena enforcement runs through the Justice Department, which answers to the same White House that wrote the memo. Any committee that wants documents will spend a year in litigation and get a contempt referral that goes nowhere. The majority that matters is the one that controls must-pass legislation, and the new defense bill is the template.

That bill already carries $400 million a year in Ukraine aid for two fiscal years and repeals the 2019 congressional sanctions on Syria's government. Those were not bipartisan side deals. They were policy outcomes that a Congress with a Democratic chamber can defend against a president who is weakened, by the briefing's own account, after Shenzhen. The lever is not oversight hearings. It is appropriations riders attached to the next funding deadline, with Ukraine and Syria as the bargaining chips and agency budgets as the penalty for stonewalling testimony.

The bold call: within six months, a Democratic chamber will hold up an agency budget line over testimony access, and the White House will concede a narrower sign-off process rather than let a shutdown fight define the midterm story. Watch Will Scharf's office and the Appropriations committees, not the judiciary panels. I have not verified the memo's text against the FT, WSJ or Reuters, so treat the exact scope as unconfirmed.

Hormuz Is the Real Tax on the AI Buildout

Iranian transit fees and a carrier surge will reprice Gulf AI capital, and export rules will follow the oil money.
EnergyFrontier Tech / AIAI Regulation & Governance
▾

Speculative: most AI-policy coverage treats the Iran war as a separate story, but the Gulf is where a large share of the sovereign capital for data centers and chip purchases sits, and that capital runs through the Strait of Hormuz. Iran has spent months requiring permission and fees for transit. Every month of that raises the risk premium on Gulf energy and makes the states that fund AI infrastructure more dependent on Washington's naval presence. Three US carriers in the region are a signal that the military cost of that dependence is rising too. I could not confirm current oil prices, so the exact figure is an open question, but the direction is clear.

The chip export angle is where the briefing's AI section and its energy section connect. The new rule limiting remote access to advanced computing chips is the most consequential AI policy in the set, and it matters more than the state-law fight. If Gulf buyers face higher energy costs and tighter access rules at the same time, the pressure lands on the licensing regime, and the likeliest outcome is case-by-case waivers for sovereign buyers in the UAE and Saudi Arabia tied to security commitments on Hormuz. That turns chip access into a bargaining chip in the same deal space as the Shenzhen trade.

Bold call: by the next Commerce licensing cycle, a large Gulf sovereign AI deal gets approved on conditions tied to shipping security, and critics in Congress frame it as a China hawk's betrayal. Watch Brent after any Hormuz headline and any new licensing announcements. This is a thesis about incentives, not a confirmed reporting finding.

SPECULATIVE ANALYSIS

The Preemption Order Is a Bluff, and California Is the Test Case

DOJ will pick procurement fights with California, not safety laws, to set a precedent it can win without Congress.
AI Regulation & GovernanceUS Policy & PowerChina
▾

Speculative: the December 2025 order cannot repeal a state AI law, and preemption legislation needs 60 Senate votes that do not exist. So the executive is left with two tools, litigation through the DOJ AI Litigation Task Force and threats to withhold federal funds. Lawyers already doubt the order can preempt states without Congress. The administration knows this. It is not trying to win a preemption case in one swing. It is trying to build a record that makes states' AI rules look like trade barriers or threats to the race with China, and the China framing is the justification that matters most.

California's assertion of authority over AI procurement is the obvious target, and it is the weakest place for a state to be challenged. Procurement is a market-participant argument, and courts are more reluctant to strike a state acting as a buyer. Colorado's repeal and replace move suggests the states that fold are the ones with the most exposure to federal grants. If the Task Force sues California, the case will be framed around national competitiveness against China, which gives the DOJ a national security story it can sell without a statute.

Bold call: the Task Force's first major filing targets California procurement rules, not a headline safety law, and Colorado's replacement law becomes the template other states copy to avoid funding fights. I found no November 2026 developments, and the EU AI Act timeline is unconfirmed, so this is a bet on the federal playbook rather than on any reported filing.

TODAY'S DEEP DIVE

Three Nuclear Powers, One Vague Statement: What Shenzhen Actually Changed

Trump, Xi and Putin's first three-way summit produced no deals, but its language on oil, immunity and nuclear risk may matter more than its lack of breakthroughs.
The trilateral summit and its joint statement came out of Shenzhen within the last day, and its follow-ups on oil sanctions, Ukraine and arms control will play out over coming weeks.

On November 19, on the sidelines of the APEC leaders' meeting in Shenzhen, Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin held the first three-way summit of the leaders of the world's three largest nuclear powers. The talks lasted a little over an hour. The three then released a roughly 250-word statement on peace, which Xinhua published. It had four points: reform of the United Nations, higher output and supply of refined petroleum products, a warning that the International Criminal Court 'must not abuse' its jurisdiction, and a commitment to dialogue on nuclear risk.

The statement does not mention Ukraine, and it does not mention the Iran war. Trump had hoped to discuss Iran, but neither Xi nor Putin raised it publicly. It sets no date or venue for the nuclear dialogue.

The summit looks thin, but the setting matters. Putin was at APEC for the first time since 2017. Russia has been diplomatically isolated since the 2022 invasion, and Putin first floated this meeting only last month. Elizabeth Wishnick of the CNA Corporation called him 'the biggest winner.' He got a photograph and a joint text alongside the American president. The text also backs state immunity for officials, which is language that protects him from the ICC warrant issued in March 2023. Trump, whose administration has sanctioned ICC officials, called the court 'a rogue globalist organization.' Washington and Moscow therefore now share a formal position on the court.

The oil language is the most consequential practical element, despite offering no volumes or timetable. At an energy-crisis moment, few governments object to a pledge to raise refined-product supply. But it appears alongside reports that Washington has moved to suspend sanctions on Russian oil, which has drawn criticism from allies. In that light, the pledge reads as political cover for easing pressure on Russia's struggling energy sector. Trump also said he and Xi share an interest in keeping oil flowing through the Strait of Hormuz, although the statement does not name the strait.

On nuclear arms, the substance is weakest. Key US-Russia treaties have expired. Trump wants China in the room, but Beijing has about 620 warheads against more than 5,000 each for the US and Russia, according to SIPRI, and it regards limits as premature. Xi's contribution was long-standing boilerplate that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought. Analysts read that as accommodating Trump's interest without committing to anything.

There are competing readings. The optimistic one is that three leaders speaking directly lowers the risk of miscalculation, and that a vague text is the price of getting any text. Yun Sun of the Stimson Center called the event well-choreographed and said vagueness is the trade-off in trilateral statements. A former US Commerce Department official said Trump can claim success, noting that Xi greeted him at the airport with a hug that Trump called 'a big win for us.' The skeptical reading is that Trump has traded legitimacy for atmospherics. Putin has ended his isolation, Xi has shown he is in the driver's seat, and Ukraine and Europe were sidelined. Zelensky called the Trump-Putin meeting a mistake. Sari Arho Havren of RUSI argues Xi 'got what he wanted.'

The summit also shows the limits of great-power choreography. Ukrainian strikes reportedly collapsed part of the Crimean Bridge roadway just before the summit, and Zelensky says Russia has hit Ukrainian energy sites more than 310 times since early November. On November 18, Iran struck Bahrain and Kuwait, its first Gulf attacks in about two months. Analysts expect China and Russia to call for peace in Iran but not to pressure Tehran. Beijing also has no interest in damaging its economic ties with Europe, which is why Ukraine stayed off the agenda.

What to watch: whether a date is set for the nuclear-risk dialogue, and whether China sends anything beyond working-level officials. Watch the formal status of US sanctions on Russian oil, and the allied reaction, in the coming weeks. The Kremlin has not confirmed Witkoff's claim that Putin agreed to let Europeans join the next round of Ukraine talks, so that is a near-term test of whether Shenzhen opened or closed space for a settlement. Finally, watch the ICC language: if Washington and Moscow cite it in future disputes, the 'peace statement' will turn out to have been less vague than it looked.
